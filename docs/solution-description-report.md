Our team has developed OFF-ROUTE, an Android navigation prototype that combines offline maps with a smartphone's motion sensors to support navigation when connectivity becomes unreliable. It provides destination routing within its downloaded map coverage and includes an inertial tracking engine that can estimate movement during a GNSS outage, provided the phone has established a valid starting position and sensor alignment. The design avoids a physical connection to the vehicle's computer or an external speedometer, making a mounted smartphone the main source of navigation data. The intended use includes delivery vehicles, taxis and two-wheelers travelling through areas where satellite reception is interrupted.

We approached the problem by separating two situations that drivers often experience together. Losing internet access affects online maps and services. Losing Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) reception affects the phone's ability to obtain a satellite-based position. Downloaded maps address the first situation. The second requires the app to estimate movement from its last reliable position, using the accelerometer and gyroscope. This process is called dead reckoning. Its accuracy depends on sensor quality, calibration and the duration of the outage, so the app also tracks uncertainty.

To develop the system, we divided the work into six connected areas: Error-State Kalman Filter design, phone calibration, AI-based speed estimation, navigation decision logic, mobile application development and map matching. Each area addresses a different source of error or part of the user experience. Their interfaces allow the team to develop individual modules while connecting them through shared sensor observations, position estimates and quality checks.

The calibration engine establishes how the mounted phone is oriented relative to the vehicle. A phone in a holder may be upright, tilted towards the driver or rotated away from the direction of travel. Its sensor axes therefore cannot be treated as the vehicle's forward, sideways and vertical directions. The engine uses gravity to establish tilt and combines suitable compass information with the direction reported by GNSS while the vehicle is moving to estimate the mounting relationship. It can then project measured acceleration onto the vehicle's forward direction.

Calibration also addresses sensor bias. A small acceleration offset can accumulate into a large speed and position error during an outage. The live engine compares accumulated forward acceleration with reliable GNSS speed changes during suitable driving periods to estimate this offset. Confirmed stops provide another opportunity to adjust acceleration and yaw-rate bias. The app captures magnetometer readings and uses Android's rotation-vector orientation, with magnetic declination correction, as a checked source of heading information.

The mounting relationship must remain valid throughout the journey. The engine checks for a significant change in gravity direction relative to the calibrated mount. When that check indicates a change, it invalidates the alignment and waits for suitable observations to establish it again. This protects against some forms of phone handling, although rotations around the vertical axis remain difficult to distinguish from vehicle turns. A stationary phone also cannot establish an unknown mounting direction from gravity alone. Secure mounting and initial alignment are therefore necessary operating conditions.

With the sensor frame established, the navigation engine can estimate movement. Our filter work includes a 15-state Error-State Kalman Filter (ESKF), implemented in Java using the EJML matrix library. It estimates errors in position, velocity, orientation, accelerometer bias and gyroscope bias. In practical terms, inertial readings predict how the vehicle has moved, while accepted observations correct that prediction according to their estimated reliability. The filter rejects inconsistent measurements and represents orientation with quaternions, which support three-dimensional rotation calculations.

The current project contains two estimation paths. The displayed position comes from the live navigation layer, which follows accepted GNSS positions when reception is healthy and uses the planar inertial estimator during outages when alignment and sensor-quality checks pass. That estimator follows forward motion and heading over the road surface. The ESKF runs as a separate research and evaluation path. Both receive sensor observations, but their roles remain distinct in the current build. This allows the team to investigate the more general fusion filter while evaluating the behaviour of the active navigation engine.

Vehicle behaviour provides additional constraints. Once the vehicle is confirmed stationary, the live estimator holds its position and sets speed to zero. The ESKF has a corresponding zero-velocity update. Its non-holonomic constraints express the expectation that an ordinarily driven vehicle has little sideways motion relative to its own direction of travel. These constraints use calibrated vehicle axes and account for slopes; they do not assume that a vehicle travelling uphill has zero vertical motion in world coordinates. Strong rotation or vibration can suppress the constraint when that assumption becomes less reliable.

The AI and speed-prediction work addresses another difficulty: consumer IMU readings contain road vibration and sensor noise alongside useful vehicle motion. The project combines signal filtering with trained models that examine a recent sequence of accelerometer and gyroscope measurements. The speed model uses a three-second, six-channel input window and a temporal convolutional neural network, which learns patterns across a sequence of readings. Its refined version uses three dilated convolutional layers and features that are less sensitive to sensor rotation. Separate neural networks estimate motion state and a proxy for GNSS agreement.

Model development uses Python with NumPy, pandas, scikit-learn and TensorFlow/Keras. The neural models are exported to TensorFlow Lite format and run locally through Google's LiteRT runtime on Android. Timestamp-based resampling provides a consistent input window despite irregular sensor callbacks. The live engine removes gravity, smooths forward acceleration and checks for shocks and stale readings. The research ESKF path also includes a Butterworth low-pass filter. Annotated pothole and phone-knock examples are absent from the training subset, and the bundled motion model's outputs for those events are fixed at zero. General shock checks therefore should not be described as a trained pothole or phone-knock detector.

The bundled models are trained models, but their current device-validation flags are false. They can run inference without being allowed to correct the live navigation estimate. The integration checks prediction freshness and agreement with reliable GNSS observations before an approved speed model can receive bounded correction weight. Successful training and export therefore do not, by themselves, enable a model to influence navigation. The current build has not demonstrated AI-based elimination of drift.

Position and speed estimates become useful to the driver through the navigation decision layer, which corresponds to the team's direction-selection work. It connects the estimated location with the chosen destination, route progress and upcoming manoeuvre. In the current implementation, GraphHopper calculates routes using car or motorcycle profiles, and the application tracks progress along those routes. This work uses routing algorithms and quality-based decision rules; the project does not currently contain a separately trained model that predicts which turn to take.

Those decisions must account for uncertain positioning. An inertial estimate drifting away from the route is insufficient evidence that the driver has taken a wrong turn. The app requires repeated, distinct, reliable GNSS observations before allowing rerouting. Route progress and precision-dependent guidance also consider the estimated uncertainty. During GNSS loss, the system can continue estimating movement only while its alignment and sensor inputs remain usable. Otherwise, it marks tracking as unavailable and retains the last estimate.

Map matching supplies the road context for this process. The project uses OpenStreetMap road data and GraphHopper's Hidden Markov Model (HMM) matcher. The matcher considers a sequence of observations and the connected road network to find a plausible road path. This helps assess the vehicle's path through bends and intersections. The live estimator accepts bounded corrections across the road direction, subject to checks on freshness, heading and residual distance. It does not use the matched road to invent forward speed.

Map matching has its own limits. Adjacent roads, flyovers and parking levels can remain ambiguous, particularly when the position estimate is already uncertain or the map lacks detail. Road matching therefore supports the estimate without establishing lane-level accuracy. The bundled offline coverage includes Madhya Pradesh roads and map features, with a more detailed central-Bhopal building overlay. Offline operation depends on the relevant map data being available; this regional coverage does not establish support for every road or indoor parking structure in India.

The mobile application brings these modules together through a React and TypeScript interface built with Vite and packaged for Android using Capacitor. Native Java components handle sensors, location observations, inference and navigation processing. Custom Capacitor plugins connect those components to the interface. MapLibre GL displays the map and route, while the application presents the vehicle marker, speed, upcoming manoeuvre, remaining distance and an offline travel-time estimate. Status and uncertainty information distinguish a recent satellite position from an inertial estimate.

A native foreground service handles ongoing sensor capture separately from the visible screen. Navigation sessions record sensor and GNSS observations locally, along with information about data quality and how the recording was produced. The interface provides journey history, sensor details, settings and diagnostic export. These records let the team investigate a reported problem using the underlying measurements, rather than relying only on a screen recording or the apparent smoothness of the marker.

For example, a journey begins with a reliable GNSS position and enough suitable observations to establish the phone's alignment. As the vehicle moves, the app updates its estimate and route progress. If GNSS becomes unavailable, the aligned inertial estimator continues from the last accepted state while uncertainty grows. Map corrections may contribute where suitable road data and accepted matches are available. When reliable GNSS returns, the engine reconciles the discrepancy progressively to reduce abrupt display jumps. Missing updates are currently detected using a freshness threshold of approximately two seconds, while explicit provider-loss events can be handled sooner. Millisecond outage detection has not been demonstrated.

The mobile service schedules position publication every 100 milliseconds, corresponding to a nominal 10 Hz output cadence. Sensor processing and model inference have separate schedules; model inference is scheduled approximately once per second. These are software operating rates. Sustained performance, power consumption and timing still require measurement across target phones.

The edge-deployable component extends the work beyond Android. It packages the shared live estimator as an Android-independent Java library for Java 17 or newer. An external application can supply timestamped acceleration, angular velocity, gravity, heading and GNSS observations through its API. It must provide consistent units and sensor axes, together with any required model runtime and road matcher. This creates a route to external IMU integration, although each sensor still needs an adapter and calibration. Tests have exercised 200 Hz input timestamps; physical fibre-optic gyroscope (FOG) integration and sustained 200 Hz hardware performance remain unverified.

Training and evaluation use the public [IO-VNBD dataset](https://github.com/onyekpeu/IO-VNBD), which contains vehicle and smartphone observations for ground-vehicle positioning research. The documented model pipeline uses 144 checksum-verified synchronised CSV files. Vehicle speed measurements provide training labels, while the speed model's input consists of IMU signals; using vehicle measurements during training does not create a requirement for a vehicle connection during inference. Whole recordings are separated for training and evaluation to reduce overlap between neighbouring samples from the same journey. The available synchronised data is at 10 Hz, and resampling cannot create higher-frequency information that was never recorded.

The refined speed model records a validation mean absolute error of 5.109 m/s, compared with 7.098 m/s for the previous model on the same validation comparison. Its error on the previously examined Driver A recordings is 3.722 m/s. Driver A is a reused regression set, so that result is not a fresh blind-test measurement. The refined motion model records 61.29% balanced accuracy on validation. These results establish preliminary model behaviour, while stationary-speed errors and limited event labels explain why the models still require device validation. The GNSS-agreement model also cannot be treated as a validated detector of actual GNSS outages.

Position evaluation is separate from speed-model evaluation. The latest saved replay summary contains fifteen injected 30-second GNSS outages. One falls below the required 10% endpoint-drift threshold, and median drift across the saved results is approximately 69.5%. Older reports and the existing trajectory plot describe different replay snapshots and must retain their own metrics. The replay excludes road matching, uses previously examined recordings and takes vehicle GNSS as its reference rather than surveyed ground truth. These results show that the drift benchmark remains unmet; they do not establish the full map-assisted system's field accuracy.

To support further development, the app records quality information that helps identify usable training examples. The desktop learning workflow accepts sufficiently fresh and accurate GNSS observations as labels and rejects unsuitable or incomplete recordings. Its laptop training agent can fit separate ridge-regression speed candidates, compare them with the existing model, and assess stopped-vehicle behaviour and outage performance. It keeps journeys separate across data splits and requires phone inference checks and controlled field review before publication. No new real-data candidate was trained, approved or published during that agent's documented implementation.

Cloud support is optional. The project includes Supabase authentication and private storage for contributed journey packages and approved model releases. Android WorkManager manages background uploads and retries, while local navigation and recording remain available without a cloud connection. The earlier Express/MongoDB service remains a separate facility for journey metadata, reports and model registration. Downloaded model updates undergo compatibility and SHA-256 integrity checks, and activation is deferred until tracking has stopped. Recovery support retains the previous model if installation fails.

The remaining acceptance work is to demonstrate the problem statement's accuracy targets through controlled drives with independent reference measurements. The requested examples are less than 5 m of drift over a 50 m GNSS outage, or less than 100 m over 1 km. Testing must cover braking, stops, turns, slopes, phone movement, motorcycle lean and GNSS recovery, alongside power consumption and sustained output rates. Lane-level positioning requires its own evidence. These measurements will determine which model and fusion changes are ready to move from the research workflow into regular navigation.
